KK60 lead selection: what 5, 10 and 20 mm actually change

Lead is the one number in a KK60 part number that trades speed against thrust against resolution. Here is the arithmetic, using the same 1 mm/rev baseline, so you can pick before you quote.

The three-way trade

For a ball screw, thrust and speed move inversely with lead at a fixed motor torque and fixed motor speed:

LeadSpeed at 3000 rpmThrust vs 5 mm leadResolution per full step (1.8° motor, direct)Pick it when
5 mm15 m/min1× (baseline)25 µmPressing, clamping, dispensing — force matters more than cycle time
10 mm30 m/min0.5×50 µmGeneral pick-and-place; the default compromise
20 mm60 m/min0.25×100 µmLong transfer moves where the load is light and the stroke is long

The numbers above are the direct consequence of lead, not a manufacturer's claim: doubling the lead doubles the distance per revolution and halves the force per unit of motor torque. The step-resolution column assumes a 1.8° stepper with no microstepping and no gearbox; add a 5:1 planetary and divide by five.

Where the 20 mm lead falls apart

Two limits show up before the thrust limit does. The first is critical speed: a longer screw whipping at high rpm. The second is resonance at the stroke ends, which appears as a positioning error that only shows up in one direction. On a 1310 mm stroke, a 20 mm lead is usually chosen for the transfer moves and then run at half the calculated top speed to stay clear of both. If your cycle needs the full 60 m/min at that stroke, you want a larger frame (KK86), not a longer KK60.

Where the 5 mm lead costs you money

15 m/min at 3000 rpm is fine for a press, painful for a gantry. Before you specify 5 mm for thrust, check whether the force you need is actually continuous or only at the moment of contact: a 10 mm lead with a 2:1 belt reduction gives you the same thrust, better resolution and a shorter cycle, at the cost of one more part in the BOM.

Mounting dimensions that do not change with lead

Rail width, combined height, hole pitch and motor flange stay the same across leads within a frame size. That is why a lead change is usually the cheapest redesign: the machine's mounting plate does not move. It is also why a "cheaper equivalent" with the same lead can still not fit — the flange is the field that breaks.
